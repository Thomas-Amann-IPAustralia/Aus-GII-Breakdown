# Glossary

Terms used in this project, in plain English. Add a term the first time the analysis uses it.

## The IP system

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **IP right** | A legal right over an idea, brand or design, granted by IP Australia: a patent, trade mark, design or plant breeder's right. In the `rights` lens, one row is one right. |
| **Patent** | Protection for an invention: up to 20 years (25 for some medicines), in return for publishing how it works. Before 1 July 1995 a standard patent lasted up to 16 years; patents still in force then got the 20 years. |
| **Standard patent** | The usual patent, examined in full before it is granted. |
| **Provisional application** | A cheap placeholder that secures a filing date for 12 months. It is never granted itself; the applicant must follow it with a full application. |
| **Innovation patent** | A cheaper, shorter (8-year) patent for smaller steps, available from 24 May 2001. It needed an *innovative step*, a lower bar than a standard patent's inventive step, and could have up to five claims. It was granted after a check of its formalities only, without examination of the invention, and could be enforced only once examined and *certified*. So an innovation patent's grant is not comparable with a standard patent's: its grant rate and time to grant say nothing about examination. It was phased out: no new ones could be filed after 25 August 2021, though divisionals and conversions of earlier applications were still allowed, and existing ones run to the end of their terms. |
| **Certification (of an innovation patent or a design)** | Examination after grant, which an innovation patent needed before its owner could enforce it. Most were never certified. IP RAPID's status "certified" marks the certified ones still in force (512 patents in October 2026). A design, too, is registered without examination of its newness and can be enforced only once examined and certified. |
| **Petty patent** | The innovation patent's predecessor: a short-term patent (up to six years) for minor inventions, available from 1979 until the innovation patent replaced it in 2001. |
| **Second-tier patent** | A petty or innovation patent: cheaper and shorter than a standard patent, for smaller steps. The lenses count them apart from standard and provisional patents. The group spans two regimes with different rules, so a series of it over time breaks in 2001, and an innovation patent's grant is not an examination (see *Innovation patent*). |
| **Trade mark** | Protection for a brand (a name, logo, sound and so on) used for particular goods or services. It can be renewed indefinitely. |
| **Design** | Protection for how a product looks (its shape, pattern or ornamentation), for up to 10 years. |
| **Plant breeder's right (PBR)** | Protection for a new plant variety. |
| **Application, registration, grant** | A right starts as an application. If it passes examination it is *registered* (trade marks, designs) or *granted* (patents). |
| **Lapse** | An *application* ends before it is granted or registered, for example because the applicant stopped paying the fees to keep it going or did not respond to examination. In IP RAPID, no lapsed right was ever granted or registered. |
| **Priority date** | The earliest filing date the application can claim, which may be from an earlier filing overseas. |
| **PCT national phase** | The step at which an international (PCT) patent application enters a country, usually about 30 months after its priority date. In IP RAPID, a PCT application's *application date* is the date it entered Australia's national phase, and its *earliest filing date* is the international filing date. |
| **Registration date of a trade mark** | A trade mark is registered "as from" its filing date, so IP RAPID's effective date for a registration is the filing date. The date the registration was actually entered is its *declared date*, which the timeline uses to count registrations by month. IP RAPID records it from 1986, so that series starts then. |
| **Grace period** | Extra time after a renewal fee falls due during which the owner can still pay (six months for trade marks and designs). A right ceases on the record only after its grace period ends, so the latest months' cessations are not yet recorded. |
| **Renewal** | Paying to keep a granted right in force for a further period. A standard patent is renewed every year from its fourth anniversary, up to 20 years; a trade mark every 10 years (every 7, then 14, under the Trade Marks Act 1955), without limit; a design once, at 5 years (Designs Act 2003). A right not renewed *ceases*. |
| **Date of patent** | The date a patent's term runs from: the filing date of its complete application. For a PCT application it is the international filing date; for a divisional, usually the earlier application's. |
| **Examination request** | A patent applicant must ask for examination (and pay for it) before the application is examined; a design's owner may ask for it after registration (see *Certification*). Trade marks are examined without being asked. |
| **Expedited examination** | Examination brought forward at the applicant's request, for example because the invention is being copied or the applicant needs the right quickly. |
| **Acceptance** | The decision that a patent or trade mark application meets the requirements. It is advertised, and then others may oppose it before the patent is granted or the mark registered. |
| **Opposition** | A formal challenge by a third party to an application (or, for trade marks, a request to remove a mark that is not being used). |
| **Assignment** | A transfer of ownership of a right, for example when a business sells its patents. |
| **Extension of term** | Up to five extra years on a patent for a pharmaceutical substance, to make up for time lost waiting for regulatory approval. |
| **Ceased** | A *granted or registered* right ends early, usually because the owner stopped paying renewal fees (or, for a trade mark, did not renew it). It signals that the owner judged the right no longer worth its cost. In IP RAPID, 99.9% of ceased patents and all ceased trade marks had been granted or registered. |
| **Attorney or agent** | A professional who files for the applicant. Applicants who file without one are *self-filers*. In the `rights` lens, `has_agent` counts only an agent who is not one of the applicants, because IP RAPID's "agent" is the address for service (ADR 0012). |
| **Founding applicant and current holder** | The founding applicant filed the right; the current holder owns it now, after any sale or transfer. In the `rights` lens, the `founder_*` columns describe who filed and the `applicant_*` columns who holds. |
| **Representation** | Who deals with IP Australia for the applicant: an *attorney* (in the lens, an agent acting for five or more applicants), *another agent* (often a related company or an in-house department), or no one (*self-filed*). |
| **Address for service** | The address IP Australia sends a right's papers to. IP RAPID lists it as the right's "agent", but it is often the applicant's own address, so an agent there is not always an attorney. |
| **Divisional application** | A new application split from an earlier one, for example to pursue part of the invention separately. It keeps the earlier application's filing date. |
| **Certification, collective and defensive trade marks** | Special kinds of trade mark. A *certification* mark shows that goods or services meet a standard; a *collective* mark is used by the members of an association; a *defensive* mark protects a very well-known mark for goods or services its owner does not trade in. |
| **Raising the Bar** | The Intellectual Property Laws Amendment (Raising the Bar) Act 2012, which tightened the standards for granting patents. Its main provisions took effect on 15 April 2013, and applicants rushed to file before then. |
| **Stage of a right** | IP RAPID's 27 statuses grouped into six: *pending* (filed, published, accepted), *in force* (granted, registered, protected, certified), *ended before grant* (lapsed, withdrawn, refused), *ended after grant* (ceased, expired, revoked, cancelled, removed), *other* (converted, linked) and *unknown* (old patents whose outcome was never recorded). |
| **IPC class** | The International Patent Classification: a code for the technology a patent covers (for example `A61K`, medical preparations). |
| **WIPO technology field** | One of 35 broad technology areas that WIPO builds from IPC classes. |
| **Nice class** | One of the 45 classes of goods and services a trade mark is registered for (for example class 35, advertising and business services). |
| **Locarno class** | The international classification for designs. |
| **PCT, Madrid, Hague** | International filing routes, for patents, trade marks and designs respectively. |
| **IP RAPID** | IP Australia's open data on every Australian IP right, updated weekly. The `rights` lens is built from it. |
| **IPLORD** | IP Australia's longitudinal research dataset, which links IP applicants to businesses. |
| **Applicant origin** | Whether a right's applicants are in Australia or overseas: *Australian* if any current applicant is in Australia, *overseas* if at least one has a known country and none is in Australia, and *origin unknown* if no applicant's country is recorded (most patents filed before 1980). |
| **Patent family** | All the filings for one invention, in every country, that share the same priority filing. One invention filed in Australia, the US and Europe is one family with several publications. |
| **Inventor** | The person who made the invention, named on the patent. The inventor can differ from the applicant (the owner), such as an employee inventing for a firm. IP RAPID does not record inventors; Google Patents does. |
| **Harmonised name** | A name tidied by Google Patents so the same person reads the same way across records. It is imperfect: the same inventor can still appear as "SMITH JOHN" on one publication and "SMITH JOHN A" on another. |

## Businesses and places

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **ABN** | Australian Business Number: the identifier that joins IP Australia's data to the business registers, the R&D Tax Incentive, AusTender and the tax data. In the `firms` lens, one row is one ABN. |
| **ABR** | The Australian Business Register, which holds every ABN. |
| **ASIC** | The Australian Securities and Investments Commission, which registers companies. |
| **GST-registered** | Registered for the Goods and Services Tax. This is compulsory at a turnover of $75,000 or more, so it is used as a rough marker of an actively trading business. |
| **R&D Tax Incentive (RDTI)** | A tax offset for research and development. The ATO publishes who claimed it and how much they spent. |
| **AusTender** | The register of Australian Government contracts. |
| **Entity type and entity group** | The ABR's legal form of the business behind an ABN (57 types, such as an Australian private company, a discretionary trading trust or an individual). The `firms` lens groups them into eight: private company, public company, sole trader, trust, partnership, government, super fund and other. |
| **Active ABN** | An ABN whose registration is current. A cancelled ABN usually means the business has closed or changed its structure. |
| **Filer and holder (of a firm's IP)** | The firm that applied for a right, and the firm that holds it now. The `firms` lens counts applications by the filer and, separately, the rights each firm holds (`n_held`). |
| **Contract notice and amendment** | AusTender publishes a notice for each contract, and another each time it is amended (CN123-A1, CN123-A2 and so on). An amendment's value is the contract's new total, not the change, so a contract's value is that of its latest notice; adding up every notice counts a contract once per amendment. |
| **ACN** | Australian Company Number: the identifier ASIC gives a company. The ABR records each company's ACN beside its ABN, which links the two. |
| **Tax transparency reports** | The ATO's published income and tax of large entities: total income of $100m or more ($200m for Australian-owned private companies until 2021-22). |
| **Postal area (POA)** | The ABS's version of a postcode area, which can be matched to Census and other data. In the `regions` lens, one row is one postal area. |
| **SA2** | Statistical Area Level 2: an ABS area of roughly 3,000 to 25,000 people. |
| **SA3, SA4** | Larger ABS areas built from SA2s: SA3s have roughly 30,000 to 130,000 people, and SA4s are larger regions (for example, Central Coast). |
| **Population Health Area (PHA)** | PHIDU's area for health statistics: one SA2, or a group of small SA2s, so that counts are large enough to publish. The `phidu/pha_sa2` table lists each PHA's SA2s. |
| **Remoteness area** | The ABS's five classes of remoteness, from Major Cities to Very Remote. |
| **SEIFA** | The ABS's Socio-Economic Indexes for Areas, built from the Census. There are four indexes:<br>- **IRSAD**: advantage and disadvantage combined.<br>- **IRSD**: disadvantage only.<br>- **IER**: economic resources.<br>- **IEO**: education and occupation.<br>A *decile* of 1 is the most disadvantaged tenth of areas, and 10 the most advantaged. |
| **Financial year (FY)** | July to June, named by the year it ends: FY2026 is July 2025 to June 2026. |
| **ANZSIC** | The Australian and New Zealand Standard Industrial Classification. Its 19 *divisions* (such as Manufacturing) are the rows of the `industries` lens. |
| **ROR** | The Research Organization Registry: an open identifier for each university, research institute and other research organisation. It keys the `institutions` lens. |
| **Consumer Price Index (CPI)** | The ABS's measure of how the prices households pay change over time, quarterly since 1948. |
| **Selected Living Cost Indexes** | ABS indexes of price change for particular kinds of household (employees, age pensioners, self-funded retirees and others). Unlike the CPI, they include mortgage interest, so they track what those households actually pay more closely. |
| **Wage Price Index** | The ABS's measure of how the price of labour (hourly pay for the same work) changes over time. |
| **Labour Force Survey** | The ABS's monthly survey of employment and unemployment. Most series come in three versions: *original* (as measured), *seasonally adjusted* (with the regular yearly pattern removed) and *trend* (smoothed further). An analysis uses one version. |
| **Owner-manager** | A person who runs their own business, incorporated or not, with or without employees. It counts people, by their main job, not businesses: a business with two owners counts twice. |
| **Real terms (deflating)** | A money amount adjusted for inflation, by dividing by a price index, so amounts from different years can be compared. The base year is always stated. |

## The analysis

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Corpus** | All the data collected for this project (`docs/catalogue.md`). |
| **Lens** | One analysis-ready table with one row per thing being studied: a right, a firm, a place, an industry in a year, a research institution in a year, a country in a year, or a month in Australia's history (the *timeline*). Every dataset is attached to at least one lens, so that it can be compared with the others. The EDA works one lens at a time. |
| **Attachment** | Bringing a dataset into a lens, by joining it on the lens's key or by summing or averaging it up to the lens's unit (for example, Census counts by postal area into `regions`). |
| **Coverage (of a series)** | The months for which a timeline series is fully recorded. Before a series' records become continuous and complete, and in final months the register is still recording, the series is left empty rather than shown as 0, so that "not recorded" is not read as "none happened". |
| **Survivors (in a register)** | The records a register still held when it was computerised. Older applications that had already failed were not carried over, so counting old filings counts only the survivors: IP RAPID's trade marks before 1983, designs before 1986 and provisional applications before September 1985. |
| **Batch dating** | Events given the date a batch of records was processed rather than the date each happened. A monthly count then shows empty months and months holding several months' worth (a *backlog*), as IP RAPID's patent cessations do before 1995. |
| **Pause and backlog** | A month with few or no events recorded, followed by one with far more, as the office catches up. Together they record the same events; apart, they look like a fall and a rush. |
| **Rush (to file)** | A jump in applications just before a rule changes, from applicants filing under the old rule while they can. |
| **Rolling 12-month total** | The sum of the last 12 months, recomputed each month. Each total holds one of every calendar month, so it shows the trend without the seasons. |
| **Seasonal profile** | Each month's count as a percentage of the 12 months centred on it, taken as the median over several years: 100 is an ordinary month, 75 a quarter below. |
| **Average growth per year (compound)** | The steady yearly rate that would take a series from its first value to its last over the years between: the constant percentage that, compounded, gives the same overall change. |
| **Event count** | A count of things the register records happening (grants, lapses, oppositions and so on), each counted once. A right can have more than one event of a kind (several oppositions, say), so event counts are not counts of rights. |
| **Standing event** | An event the register still treats as in effect. An event later reversed (a lapse undone by an extension of time, say) stays in IP RAPID but is not standing. |
| **Lifetime (years in force)** | How long a granted right stayed in force: from the date its term runs from to the day it ended. It measures how long owners judged the right worth its fees, which is one sign of its private value, not the value itself. |
| **Unfinished (censored)** | A lifetime still running when the data end: the right is still in force, so all that is known is that it will last at least this long. Treating it as ended, or leaving it out, would make rights look shorter-lived than they are. |
| **Survival curve (Kaplan-Meier)** | The share of a group still going (here, still in force) at each age, worked out so that unfinished lifetimes count for as long as they are known and no longer. A step down marks an age at which many rights end at once, such as a renewal date. |
| **Cohort** | A group that starts at the same time, such as the trade marks filed in the 1990s. Comparing cohorts at the same age separates change over time from ageing. |
| **Observation status** | A publisher's flag on a single value: *estimated*, *provisional* (likely to be revised), *definition differs* (from the standard one) or a *time series break* (a change of method, so values either side are not directly comparable). The national lens keeps the OECD's in `status`. |
| **Reprocessing** | Rebuilding a corpus table from the raw files already stored, without fetching anything new, so that a parser fix changes the table but not the date of the data (`corpus reprocess`). |
| **Unit of analysis** | What one row stands for. Counting or averaging only makes sense once this is clear. |
| **EDA** | Exploratory data analysis: getting to know the data before trying to answer questions with it. |
| **Univariate, bivariate, multivariate** | Looking at one variable at a time, at two together, or at several together. These are the EDA's three stages. |
| **Distribution** | How the values of a variable are spread out: which values are common and which are rare. |
| **Mean and median** | The average, and the middle value. When a few very large values pull the mean up, the median is the better guide to a "typical" value. |
| **Skew** | Lopsidedness. *Right-skewed* data has a long tail of large values, as with most counts of IP filings, where a few firms file thousands of applications and most file one. |
| **Outlier** | A value far from the rest. Here this follows the box-plot rule: more than 1.5 times the middle half's width beyond the middle half. An outlier is not necessarily an error. |
| **Box plot** | A chart of the middle half of the values (the box), the median (the line inside it) and the outliers (the dots). |
| **Middle half (interquartile range, IQR)** | The range from the 25th to the 75th percentile, which holds the middle 50% of the values. A box plot draws it as the box. |
| **Bin** | One bar of a histogram: the values within a range (say, 100 to 200 days) counted together. |
| **KDE** | A smoothed outline of a histogram. |
| **Log scale** | An axis where each step is ten times the last, so very small and very large values fit on one chart. |
| **Binning artefact** | A peak or gap that appears only because of how a histogram's bars were drawn. It is checked by redrawing with different bar widths. |
| **Correlation (Pearson r)** | A number from -1 to 1 for how closely two variables follow a straight line. 0 means no straight-line link. |
| **Spearman rho** | Like r, but it asks only whether one variable rises as the other rises, in any shape. It is robust to extreme values. |
| **LOWESS** | A smooth trend line that follows the data without assuming it is straight. |
| **p-value** | How surprising a result would be if there were no real link. With very large samples, tiny and unimportant links still get tiny p-values, so the size of the link matters more. |
| **Effect size** | How big a difference or link is, as opposed to whether it is "significant". |
| **95% interval** | The range that plausibly contains the true value. Rates use the Wilson interval, which behaves well for small groups and rates near 0% or 100%. |
| **Mann-Whitney, Kruskal-Wallis** | Tests of whether groups differ (two groups, or more). They work on ranks, so a few extreme values can't drive the result. |
| **Confounder** | A third factor that drives two things and makes them look linked. For example, firm size drives both R&D and patenting. |
| **Partial correlation** | The link between two variables after removing what one or more other variables explain. |
| **PCA** | Principal component analysis: it combines many related variables into a few summary measures. |
| **VIF** | Variance inflation factor: how far a variable can be predicted from the others. Above about 5 to 10, the variables are repeating one another. |
| **Clustering (k-means)** | Sorting rows into groups that look alike across several variables. The *silhouette* score (-1 to 1) says how well separated the groups are. |
| **Trend** | The long-run direction of a series over years, ignoring short ups and downs. |
| **Seasonality** | A pattern that repeats every year, such as fewer applications over the Christmas holidays. |
| **Moving average** | The average of the last few values (here, usually 12 months), redrawn each month. It smooths out seasons and one-off spikes. |
| **Structural break** | A point where a series jumps or changes direction, for example when a rule changes. |
| **Interrupted time series** | A way to measure a break: the series' trend and season before a change are used to estimate what would have happened without it, and compared with what did. |
| **Placebo test** | Running the same analysis at a date when nothing happened. If it finds "breaks" there too, a break at the real date is less convincing. |
| **STL decomposition** | A method that splits a series into its trend, its seasonal pattern and what is left over. |
| **Spurious correlation** | A link with no real connection behind it. It is common between time series that all trend upward over the same years. |
| **Multiple comparisons** | When many pairs are tested at once, some look strong by chance alone. Stage 2 corrects for the number of pairs screened, and keeps a lead only if it also holds in a second period or sample. |
| **Strength of evidence** | Every observation is labelled with one of four strengths, from the research goal:<br>- **descriptive**: what the data shows;<br>- **observational**: a link that may have other explanations;<br>- **causal**: an effect, shown by a suitable design;<br>- **projected**: an estimate of the future. |
| **Additionality** | What happens *because of* a right or an intervention, compared with what would have happened anyway. It is the research goal's central test. |
| **Natural experiment** | A change (a new fee or rule, say) that affects some people and not others, for reasons unrelated to their outcomes. Comparing the two groups comes close to an experiment. |
| **Estimated gender** | The probability that a person is a woman, worked out from their first name and country with a name-gender dictionary (WGND). It is not anyone's stated gender. Added up over many people, it gives an *expected count* of women and men, which is reliable for large groups but not for any one person. |
| **Lower bound** | A count that is at least this large; the true figure may be higher. |
| **Concentration (top 1% share)** | The share of a total held by the largest 1% of those who hold any of it. If the top 1% of filing firms file 55% of standard patents, a few hundred firms account for more than half. |
| **Nominal dollars** | Money as recorded at the time, not adjusted for inflation (see *Real terms*). |
| **Placeholder date** | A made-up date standing in for an unknown one, such as 1 January 1970 (the zero of many computer systems) or 1 January 1800. Placeholder dates are emptied, not counted. |
| **Decided (applications)** | Applications whose outcome is known: granted or registered, or ended. Rates and times are compared only for years in which nearly all applications are decided, because the ones still pending would otherwise be missed. |
| **Left-truncated (age)** | An age measured from a date that cannot be earlier than some cut-off, so older units all look as young as the cut-off allows. A business first seen through its ABN or GST date in 1999 or 2000 may be much older: ABNs and GST began then. |
