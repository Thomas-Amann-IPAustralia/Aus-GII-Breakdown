
Research Design: Maximising Public Value from Australia's Intellectual Property System

Proposed research programme for IP Australia

Status: Draft for discussion

1. Executive summary

This research programme will investigate how Australia's intellectual property system contributes to innovation, economic prosperity and broader public welfare, and identify where government intervention could generate the greatest additional public value.

It will examine the relationship between intellectual property rights, innovation activity, business outcomes and societal outcomes, while investigating how these relationships vary across industries, regions, population groups and stages of business development.

The research will not assume that increasing IP registration, innovation activity or any particular economic indicator is inherently desirable. Instead, it will investigate the circumstances in which different forms of intellectual property protection create value, the conditions that enable that value to be realised, and the costs, barriers and unintended consequences that may arise.

The ultimate objective is to inform IP Australia's strategic priorities by identifying:

- Opportunities: Where targeted intervention could generate substantial additional public value.
- Gaps: Where the existing system fails to meet the needs of particular groups, industries or innovation activities.
- Levers: Which policy, service, regulatory, funding or partnership interventions could influence outcomes.
- Distribution: Who benefits from the existing system, who bears its costs, and who may be underserved.
- Expected impact: What outcomes different interventions could reasonably be expected to produce, over what timeframe, and with what degree of confidence.
- Strategic choices: Where IP Australia should concentrate resources, develop new capabilities, collaborate with other institutions or advocate for changes beyond its direct remit.

The programme will combine quantitative analysis, qualitative research, causal inference where feasible, international evidence and forward-looking scenario analysis. Findings will be presented with explicit distinctions between established evidence, plausible hypotheses and speculative possibilities.

2. Research purpose and scope

2.1 Primary objective

To develop an evidence-based understanding of how Australia's intellectual property system contributes to public value, and to identify the interventions through which IP Australia could most effectively improve innovation and societal outcomes.

2.2 Research principles

The research will be guided by six principles.

Public value over activity measures. Application volumes, registrations, service utilisation and other administrative measures are useful indicators of activity, but are not sufficient measures of success.

Additionality over attribution. The central question is what additional benefit occurs because a right, policy, service or intervention exists, compared with what would otherwise have happened.

Pluralism over a single definition of innovation. Innovation may include scientific invention, new products and processes, business-model innovation, creative activity, diffusion and adoption of existing technologies. The research will not equate innovation exclusively with patenting or R&D.

Distribution over national averages alone. Aggregate benefits may conceal unequal access, concentrated gains or adverse effects on particular groups.

Openness to disconfirming evidence. The research will test the possibility that some forms of registration are beneficial in particular circumstances, unnecessary in others, or associated with net costs.

Evidence proportionate to the claim. Exploratory correlations, observational estimates, causal effects and future projections will be clearly distinguished.

2.3 Scope

The initial investigation will focus on Australia's registered IP system, including patents, trade marks and registered designs, while considering copyright and other mechanisms where they provide useful comparisons.

It will examine both the system's direct effects and its interaction with the wider innovation ecosystem, including research institutions, finance, education, skills, entrepreneurship, commercialisation, competition and knowledge diffusion.

The programme will consider interventions within IP Australia's remit as well as opportunities requiring collaboration with other agencies, research institutions, industry, investors or governments.

It is not intended to be an audit of the efficacy of IP Australia's existing public education and awareness activities. Those activities may be considered as one possible policy lever, alongside alternative and complementary interventions.

3. Core research questions

Question 1: What public value does the IP system create?

- What economic, social, technological and environmental outcomes are associated with different forms of IP protection?
- Under what circumstances do patents, trade marks and registered designs support innovation, commercialisation, investment, competition or knowledge diffusion?
- What are the costs of obtaining, maintaining, licensing and enforcing rights?
- What are the potential costs to consumers, competitors, follow-on innovators and the broader public?
- What can existing evidence establish about the system's net contribution to public welfare?

Question 2: Who benefits, and who is underserved?

- Which businesses, industries, regions and population groups derive the greatest benefits from IP protection?
- Which groups face disproportionate barriers to understanding, accessing or using the system?
- Are there underserved groups with substantial unrealised innovation potential?
- How do outcomes differ between startups, SMEs, established businesses, research institutions and larger enterprises?
- How do outcomes vary by gender, geography, socioeconomic circumstances and other relevant characteristics, where reliable and appropriate data are available?

Question 3: What determines whether IP protection produces value?

- Which business, institutional and market conditions enable rights holders to commercialise or otherwise benefit from their IP?
- How important are complementary capabilities such as finance, skills, management, market access and collaboration?
- Does formal registration change access to investment, licensing, procurement, partnerships or international markets?
- When do alternative approaches, such as trade secrets, open licensing, collaborative research or public funding, produce better outcomes?
- What distinguishes successful use of registered IP from registration that produces little observable benefit?

Question 4: Where are the greatest opportunities for intervention?

- Which barriers prevent potentially valuable innovation from occurring or reaching the market?
- Which barriers could IP Australia materially influence?
- Where might targeted funding, new services, regulatory changes, partnerships or improved access to expertise generate additional public value?
- Which interventions could address market failures or institutional gaps that existing arrangements do not adequately address?
- Where should IP Australia focus its own efforts, and where would collaboration or advocacy be more appropriate?

Question 5: What outcomes could interventions produce?

- What is the expected magnitude, distribution and timing of the benefits?
- How much would be additional to what would occur without intervention?
- What are the likely financial, administrative and opportunity costs?
- How sensitive are expected results to assumptions about participation, effectiveness, economic conditions and implementation?
- What evidence would be needed to justify scaling, modifying or discontinuing an intervention?

4. Conceptual framework

The research will distinguish five connected but separate concepts.

1. Innovation potential: The capabilities, ideas, research, skills and opportunities from which innovation may emerge.
2. Innovation activity: The development, adoption, diffusion and application of new or significantly improved products, processes and other solutions.
3. IP protection and use: The acquisition, management, licensing, enforcement and strategic use of registered rights and other protection mechanisms.
4. Private and organisational outcomes: Commercialisation, productivity, revenue, employment, investment, resilience and other outcomes experienced by innovators and organisations.
5. Public value: The net effects on economic prosperity, social progress, opportunity, environmental sustainability, competition and public welfare.

The relationships between these concepts will be treated as empirical questions rather than assumed causal relationships.

For example, registration may support commercialisation, but the effect may depend on access to capital, market conditions and the ability to enforce or license a right. Innovation may produce private returns without producing equivalent public benefits. Conversely, an innovation may create substantial public value without generating a patent or other registered right.

The framework will therefore investigate both the mechanisms through which IP protection creates value and the circumstances in which those mechanisms fail to operate.

Proposed guiding question:
Where, how and for whom can IP Australia's interventions generate the greatest additional public value, and what evidence would justify acting on those opportunities?
